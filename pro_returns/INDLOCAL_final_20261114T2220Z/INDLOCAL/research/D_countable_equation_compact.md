# Countable equation-compact targets do not detect the Higman core

**Status (14 November 2026): proved.** This note closes the proposal to use a
countable, fully equation-compact target containing the Higman-based homology
core. The argument does not produce a presentation with invertible relative
exponent matrix for an arbitrary remaining target. Its centralizer equations
have zero exponent rows; no conversion of those rows is asserted.

## 1. Exact statement

Let

\[
 J=\langle a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3\mid
 a_i a_{i+1}a_i^{-1}=a_{i+1}^{2}\quad(i\bmod4)\rangle.
\]

Every homomorphism from \(J\) to a countable equation-compact group is trivial.
Here equation compactness means that every system of group equations with
coefficients in the group, each finite subsystem of which has a solution in
the group, has a simultaneous solution in the group. It suffices to require
this for countable systems of equations with countably many unknowns.

The same vanishing conclusion holds for any group normally generated by the
images of homomorphisms from copies of \(J\). Indeed a homomorphism out of such
a group kills each of those images, and its kernel is normal, so it kills
their normal closure. This is the exact propagation used for the already
constructed Higman-based homology core and its normally generated extensions.

## 2. Countable systems suffice for a countable coefficient group

For completeness, let \(E\) be countable and suppose every finitely solvable
countable system over \(E\) is solvable in \(E\). Let \(\Sigma\) be an arbitrary
finitely solvable system. Well-order its unknowns and assign them one at a
time, maintaining finite solvability of the equations after substitution of
all previously assigned values.

At a successor step, write \(x\) for the next unknown. For every finite
subsystem of the currently substituted equations, existentially quantify all
its unknowns except \(x\). The resulting conditions on \(x\) are positive
primitive formulas with coefficients in \(E\). There are only countably many
such formulas up to renaming their finitely many bound variables: each is a
finite string in a countable alphabet. Give the existential variables of
different formulas disjoint names. Their conjunction is thus a countable
system of equations. Every finite part is solvable, by finite solvability of
the original residual system. A solution supplies a value of \(x\) that
preserves finite solvability of that whole residual system.

At a limit step every finite subsystem involves only finitely many already
assigned unknowns, whose values were fixed before some earlier step. Thus
finite solvability persists. Transfinite recursion assigns every unknown and
gives a solution of \(\Sigma\). This proves full equation compactness.

This is also the group instance of Mycielski--Ryll-Nardzewski, Theorem 4,
pp. 274--275; the direct proof above specifies the reduction needed here.

## 3. A descending-chain consequence

A countable equation-compact group \(E\) has no infinite strictly descending
chain of centralizers

\[
 C_0>C_1>C_2>\cdots.
\]

To prove this, choose \(u_n\in C_n\setminus C_{n+1}\). Recursively split a
left coset \(gC_n\) into the two disjoint subcosets
\(gC_{n+1}\) and \(gu_nC_{n+1}\). Each branch of the resulting binary tree is
a nested sequence of nonempty cosets. If \(C_n=C_E(S_n)\), membership in
\(gC_n\) is exactly the equations

\[
 [g^{-1}x,s]=1\qquad(s\in S_n).
\]

The branch equations are finitely solvable. Equation compactness realizes
each branch. Distinct branches have disjoint realizations, so they give an
injection of \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\) into \(E\), contradicting countability.
All the systems used for an individual branch are countable, because all
coefficients belong to the countable group \(E\).

The identical coset argument proves the descending-chain condition for
subgroups defined by positive primitive formulas, or by systems of
equations with additional existential unknowns: use fresh witness unknowns
for each membership condition. We will apply this only to the subgroups
\(n!A\) of a countable equation-compact abelian group \(A\), where membership
is the single equation \(x=n!y\).

## 4. An infinite-order doubling pair forces a rational affine subgroup

Suppose \(a,b\in E\) satisfy

\[
 bab^{-1}=a^2
\]

and \(a\) has infinite order. Put \(C=C_E(a)\). Then

\[
 bCb^{-1}=C_E(a^2)\supseteq C.
\]

Consequently \(b^{-n}Cb^n\) is a descending sequence of centralizers. Its
stabilization, conjugated back by a suitable power of \(b\), gives
\(bCb^{-1}=C\).

The abelian group \(Z(C)=C_E(C)\) is therefore normalized by \(b\). In additive
notation define

\[
 A=\{x\in Z(C):bxb^{-1}=2x\}.
\]

This is an abelian subgroup, it contains \(a\), and it is preserved by
conjugation by both \(b\) and \(b^{-1}\). It is defined within \(E\) by the
equations \([x,c]=1\) for every \(c\in C\), together with the displayed
doubling equation. It is itself equation-compact: to a finitely solvable
system over \(A\), add these membership equations for each unknown. Every
finite part of the enlarged system is solvable in \(A\); an \(E\)-solution of
the whole enlarged system lies in \(A\).

By Section 3 the chain \(A\supseteq2!A\supseteq3!A\supseteq\cdots\)
stabilizes. Write \(M=N!A\) for its stable value. The group \(M\) is divisible.
Indeed, for \(z\in M\) and a positive integer \(m\), choose \(k\ge N\) with
\(mN!\mid k!\), write \(z=k!w\), and observe that

\[
 z=m\left(N!\frac{k!}{mN!}w\right),
 \qquad N!\frac{k!}{mN!}w\in M.
\]

The element \(a'=N!a\) still has infinite order. Choose \(v_1=a'\) and,
recursively, \((n+1)v_{n+1}=v_n\) in \(M\). The rule

\[
 \iota\left(\frac{m}{n!}\right)=mv_n
\]

defines a homomorphism \(\iota:\mathbb Q\to M\): the compatibility equations
verify equality under passage to a common factorial denominator. It is
injective since \(n!v_n=a'\) and \(a'\) has infinite order. Conjugation by
\(b\) multiplies every element of this copy of \(\mathbb Q\) by 2.

The map

\[
 \mathbb Q\rtimes_{\times2}\mathbb Z\longrightarrow E,
 \qquad(q,k)\longmapsto\iota(q)b^k
\]

is therefore a homomorphism. It is injective: if \(b^k\in\iota(\mathbb Q)\),
then \(b^k\) centralizes that nonzero rational subgroup, while its actual
conjugation action is multiplication by \(2^k\). Hence \(k=0\), after which
injectivity of \(\iota\) gives \(q=0\). This proves the stated embedding, with
its exact morphism.

## 5. The rational affine subgroup has no equation-compact overgroup

Here is the short original obstruction, with all evaluations explicit.
Suppose an equation-compact group \(E\) contains elements \(t(q)\),
\(q\in\mathbb Q\), and \(b\) with

\[
 t(q)t(r)=t(q+r),\qquad bt(q)b^{-1}=t(2q),\qquad t(1)\ne1.
\]

Choose a set \(S\) with \(|S|>|E|\). Introduce unknowns \(x_s\) for \(s\in S\)
and \(y_{st}\) for ordered pairs \(s\ne t\), and impose

\[
 [y_{st},x_sx_t^{-1}]=t(1)\qquad(s\ne t),
 \tag{*}
\]

using \([u,v]=uvu^{-1}v^{-1}\). Every finite subsystem has a solution in the
displayed affine subgroup. Assign distinct integers \(n_s\) to its finitely
many indices, set \(x_s=b^{n_s}\), and set

\[
 y_{st}=t\left(\frac{1}{1-2^{n_s-n_t}}\right).
\]

Indeed \([t(r),b^m]=t((1-2^m)r)\), so every required equation holds. A full
solution of (*) would force \(x_s\ne x_t\) for every distinct pair, since
otherwise the left side would equal 1. That would inject \(S\) into \(E\),
a contradiction.

This is precisely Example E3 on pp. 276--277 of Mycielski--Ryll-Nardzewski
(printed E2 and renumbered E3 in the published erratum),
with the affine maps \(w\mapsto2^kw+r\). The argument uses only this doubling
subgroup, not the full group of rational affine maps with arbitrary nonzero
rational slope. The full original proof was checked directly from the scan.

## 6. The Higman conclusion

Sections 4 and 5 show that every element in a countable equation-compact
group which is conjugate to its square has finite order. Thus the images of
the four generators of \(J\) have finite order.

For clarity, the elementary finite-order argument is as follows. If some
generator image has order greater than one, choose the smallest prime \(p\)
dividing the order of any generator image. The doubling relation implies
that all generator orders are odd, so \(p>2\). Choose an adjacent pair
\(u,v\) with \(uvu^{-1}=v^2\) and \(p\mid |v|\). Then the multiplicative order
of \(2\) modulo \(p\) divides \(|u|\). It is greater than one and divides
\(p-1\), so one of its prime divisors is smaller than \(p\) and divides
\(|u|\), contradicting the choice of \(p\). Every generator image is
therefore trivial.

DOI [10.4064/fm-62-3-309](https://doi.org/10.4064/fm-62-3-309).

<https://matwbn.icm.edu.pl/ksiazki/fm/fm62/fm62122.pdf>.
It has been read visually by two agents. The corrections on p. 276 change
the typeface of the group and translation-group symbols, change a lower-case
index to the set index in a product, renumber E2 to E3, and remove parentheses
around the phrase "at most denumerable". They make no change to the affine
group, the equation (*), or its proof. The remaining errata concern other
passages. The explicit affine-system argument in Section 5 has also been
verified directly.

Gunter H. Wenzel's authored Appendix 6, *Equational Compactness*, in
Gratzer's *Universal Algebra*, second edition (1979), pp. 417--447, also
explicitly reports the doubling subgroup on p. 436. That later report was
used to locate the primary proof; the proof above is fully specified.

The first unproved step in applying this route to the unresolved cardinal
problem remains exact: no presentation with invertible relative ordinary
boundary has been constructed that forces the centralizer and affine
compactness equations for an arbitrary surviving countable target. The
equations in (*) have zero exponent rows. Adding a fresh pivot to each row
and then forcing those pivots to be trivial creates dependent rows; it does
not supply the required integral direct-sum isomorphism.
